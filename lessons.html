Lesson 1

Animation Through Ages

Meaning of Animation

Anima is a Latin root meaning “breath,” “soul,” or “vital force.” Animatio is a Latin word meaning “giving life.” Animation is the art of creating the illusion of movement by displaying a sequence of images, drawings, or objects in rapid succession.

History and Development

Pre-Cinematic Era — Before the 1900s

Optical toys, shadow plays, and mechanical devices created the illusion of movement using sequential images. Some historians believe prehistoric cave paintings showing animals with multiple legs may have been early attempts to depict motion.

Thaumatrope (1825)

A simple toy consisting of a disk with different images on each side, attached to strings.

Phenakistoscope (1832)

  • Created by Joseph Plateau.
  • A spinning disk with images arranged in a sequence.
  • When viewed through slits while rotating, the images appeared to move.

Zoetrope (1834)

A cylindrical device with a series of drawings inside. When spun and viewed through slits, the images created the illusion of motion.

Flipbook (1868)

Contains a sequence of drawings on separate pages. Flipping the pages rapidly creates the appearance of movement.

Early Animation Era — 1900s–1920s

As motion-picture technology developed, animators began creating animated films. In frame-by-frame animation, artists drew images individually and photographed them one frame at a time.

Émile Cohl is known as the “Father of Animated Cartoons.” He created Fantasmagorie (1908), considered one of the earliest fully animated films.

Golden Age of Animation — 1920s–1960s

Major developments included the introduction of sound, the introduction of color, and character animation. Walt Disney was a notable pioneer who revolutionized animation through storytelling, sound, and technological innovation.

Important films listed in the lesson include Steamboat Willie, Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, Pinocchio, and Fantasia.

Steamboat Willie (1928)

A short animated film that introduced Mickey Mouse to a wide audience. Mickey works on a steamboat under the strict Captain Pete. After getting into trouble, Mickey entertains himself by making music using animals and objects on the boat. The cartoon became famous for its synchronized sound and was a major milestone in animation history.

Fantasia (1940)

Fantasia combines classical music with imaginative animated stories. Rather than one continuous plot, it has several segments, each set to a different piece of classical music. Memorable scenes include Mickey Mouse as the Sorcerer’s Apprentice, dancing hippos and ostriches, and a dramatic struggle between good and evil. The film is known for its artistic animation and creative way of bringing music to life.

Television Animation Era — 1960s–1990s

As televisions became common in households, animation shifted from movie theaters to television screens.

Digital Animation Era — 1990s–Present

Artists began using software instead of drawing every frame manually. 3D animation allowed characters and environments to appear more realistic. Toy Story was the first fully computer-generated feature-length animated film and marked a major milestone in animation history.

Contemporary and Future Animation

  • Motion capture: Artists’ movements are recorded and transferred to digital characters.
  • Virtual reality (VR): Lets users interact with animated environments in immersive experiences.
  • Artificial intelligence (AI) tools: Assist in generating animations, improving workflows, and creating realistic character movements.
  • Real-time animation: Used in video games and virtual productions, where animation is generated instantly.

The lesson timeline also mentions theatrical animation, television, CGI, digital ink, streaming, virtual reality, and real-time tools as developments across the eras.

Example in everyday animation

A flipbook showing a ball moving from left to right uses a sequence of slightly different drawings. When you flip the pages quickly, your eyes read the drawings as one moving action. This demonstrates the basic idea behind animation.

Lesson 2

Launch Your Animation Studio

What Is an Animation Studio?

An animation studio is a company or organization that creates animated media. It combines art, storytelling, technology, and business.

Main Functions

  • Develops story ideas and concepts.
  • Designs characters, backgrounds, and visual assets.
  • Produces animation sequences.
  • Edits audio, music, and visual effects.
  • Publishes or delivers the final animated product.

Vision

To entertain, inform, and inspire people around the globe through the power of unparalleled storytelling.

Mission

To be one of the world’s leading producers and providers of entertainment and information.

Studio Name and Brand Identity

A studio name should be:

  • Memorable.
  • Easy to pronounce.
  • Unique.
  • Related to creativity or animation.

Brand identity may include:

  • Studio logo.
  • Color palette.
  • Typography.
  • Slogan or tagline.

Target Audiences

  • Children.
  • Teenagers.
  • Businesses.
  • Schools.

Studio Production Team

  • Director.
  • Storyboard artist.
  • Animator.
  • Character designer.
  • Producer.
  • Editor and sound designer.

Possible Sources of Work

  • Local businesses.
  • Schools and universities.
  • Social media creators.
  • Government information campaigns.
  • YouTube channels.
  • Freelance platforms.

Responsible and Ethical Practices

  • Use original artwork.
  • Respect copyright and intellectual property.
  • Give proper credit to team members.
  • Meet deadlines responsibly.
  • Communicate professionally with clients.
  • Protect project files and data.
Studio example

A small studio receives a request from a school to make a 30-second animated safety reminder. The director plans the message, the storyboard artist maps each scene, the character designer prepares the characters, the animator creates movement, and the editor combines visuals and sound.

Lesson 3

Visual Elements and Principles of Design

Key Terms

  • Graphic: Visual; associated with art, imagination, and expression.
  • Visual elements: The building blocks of design.
  • Design: The process of organizing visual elements to communicate an idea effectively and creatively.
  • Principles of design: Rules that help organize design elements to make a design clear, balanced, and visually appealing.
  • Graphic design: The art and practice of using visual elements and design principles to create attractive and meaningful designs.

Visual Elements

Line

A continuous mark on a surface.

Shape

A two-dimensional enclosed space formed by a line.

  • Geometric: Regular shapes.
  • Organic: Free-form shapes.

Form

Exists in three dimensions, with height, width, and depth.

Color

A visual element created by light that makes objects appear different and enhances the overall design.

Space

The area around, above, below, or behind an object.

  • Positive space.
  • Negative space.

Texture

The physical quality of a surface. It can be smooth, rough, hard, or soft.

Typography

The art of arranging and styling text to make it clear, readable, and visually appealing.

Principles of Design

  • Balance: The distribution of visual weight.
    • Symmetrical balance.
    • Asymmetrical balance.
  • Alignment: An arrangement forming a straight line.
  • Emphasis: An accentuation of importance.
  • Proportion: The relative size of the components that compose an object, including size, color, quantity, or degree.
  • Movement: A directed path of optical motion.
  • Pattern: An orderly repetition of objects.
  • Contrast: A comparison that accentuates difference.
Design example

For a poster about protecting the environment, green may communicate nature, a large title creates emphasis, aligned text improves organization, and empty space keeps the design readable. These choices show how visual elements work with design principles.

Lesson 4

Colors and Typography

Color

Color is the appearance something has as a result of reflecting light. Color can be symbolic, create a specific look and feel, give emphasis, and give life to an object.

  • Hue: The dominant color family.
  • Color: A general term used to describe every hue.
  • Color wheel: An illustrative model showing color hues around a circle.

Color Groups

  • Primary colors: Red, blue, and yellow.
  • Secondary colors: Made by mixing primary colors.
  • Tertiary colors: Made by mixing primary and secondary colors.
  • Warm colors: Red, orange, and yellow, including variations of those colors.
  • Cool colors: Green, blue, and purple, including variations of those colors.
  • Neutral colors: Black, white, gray, tans, and browns.

Color Models

  • RGB: Red, Green, and Blue.
  • CMYK: Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, and Key (Black).
  • Hex codes: Used to name colors in web design.

Color Rules and Schemes

  • Monochromatic: A color scheme made up of variations of one color.
  • Analogous: Colors grouped because they are similar and have close relationships to one another.
  • Complementary: Colors found on opposite ends of the color wheel.
  • Triadic: A scheme using three colors paired to create a vibrant and playful feel.

Typography

Typography is the art of arranging letters and text so they are clear and visually appealing to the reader. It also refers to the style or appearance of text.

Typeface: The design of letters, numbers, and other symbols used in print or electronic display. Typeface helps set the intended look, feel, tone, and character of a piece of work.

Typography Cases

  • Sentence case.
  • UPPER CASE.
  • lower case.
  • Title Case.

Typeface Categories

  • Serif: Has small strokes, called serifs, attached to the main parts of letters.
  • Sans serif: Does not have extra strokes. “Sans serif” means “without serif” in French.
  • Display: Comes in many different styles.

Typography Spacing and Hierarchy

  • Hierarchy: Guides the reader’s eye to whatever is most important.
  • Leading: Space between lines of text, also called line spacing.
  • Tracking: Overall space between characters, also called character spacing.
  • Kerning: Space between specific characters.

These are used to create professional-looking designs.

Color-Related Terms

  • Melanophobia: Fear of the color black.
  • Leukophobia: Fear of the color white.
Color and typography example

A cheerful children’s animation poster might use a warm analogous palette such as yellow, orange, and red. A bold display typeface can make the title stand out, while a simple sans serif typeface keeps the details easy to read.

Lesson 5

Image Formats and Camera Angles

Pixels and Resolution

  • Pixel: “Picture element”; the smallest unit of a digital image or graphic.
  • Resolution: The total number of pixels in a digital image.
  • Megapixel (MP): A unit of measurement equivalent to 1,000,000 pixels. One megapixel equals 1,000,000 pixels.
📐 Megapixel Formula

Megapixels = (Pixel Height × Pixel Width) ÷ 1,000,000

How to Check Image Dimensions

  1. Select a picture.
  2. Right-click it or choose its settings.
  3. Select Properties, then choose Details.
  4. Find the pixel height and pixel width.

Raster and Vector Graphics

Raster graphics: Also known as bitmap graphics. Image information is rendered as pixels.

Examples or uses: photos, digital art, and web graphics.

Vector graphics: Geometry-based graphics created using mathematical formulas that define points, lines, and curves instead of relying on a grid of pixels.

Examples or uses: logos, icons, and fonts.

Image File Formats

  • .JPEG: Joint Photographic Experts Group; a standard format for containing lossy, compressed image data.
  • .HEIC: High Efficiency File Format; traditionally used by Apple.
  • .PNG: Portable Network Graphics; supports a transparent background.
  • .GIF: Graphics Interchange Format; supports animation.
Worked example: Megapixels

An image measures 2,000 pixels wide and 1,500 pixels high.

(2,000 × 1,500) ÷ 1,000,000 = 3 megapixels

Choosing a file format

Use JPEG for a compressed photograph, PNG when you need a transparent background, GIF for a simple animated image, and HEIC when working with compatible photos from many Apple devices.

Lesson 6

Storyboards, Framing, and Camera Shots

Storyboard

A storyboard is a visual representation of a film sequence. It breaks down the action into individual panels.

Framing Height

  • A guide for accurate cinematography and photography.
  • Helps with subject framing and frame cropping.

Camera Shot

A shot is the basic unit of film language. It can portray things about a character, setting, or theme to the audience.

Camera Shots According to Size

  • Close-up shot: Shows the actor from the shoulders up to the head.
  • Medium shot: Shows the subject from the waist up.
  • Full shot: Shows the character’s body and reveals where the actor is positioned.
  • Long shot: Similar to a full shot but covers a wider area.

Camera Shots According to Position on the Screen

  • Master shot: Keeps all characters in view and shows the action of a scene from start to finish.
  • Establishing shot: Provides an overview of the setting.
  • Point-of-view shot: A subjective shot showing the scene the way a character sees it.
  • Reaction shot: Shows a reaction to something or someone.
  • Over-the-shoulder shot: Used to facilitate conversation between two people.
Storyboard example

For a scene where a student enters a classroom, an establishing shot first shows the classroom, a medium shot follows the student walking in, a close-up shows the student’s worried expression, and a reaction shot shows a classmate responding.
